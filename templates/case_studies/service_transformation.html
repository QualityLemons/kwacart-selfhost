Case study · Service transformation

Finding the hidden causes of delay across an entire service

A cross-functional team used Shift & Share to compare six improvement experiments—and discovered that waiting, handoffs and bottlenecks mattered more than how quickly people completed the work itself.

The challenge

Move beyond the assumption that people simply needed to work faster

Delays were visible, but their causes were not. Work arrived incomplete, status was hard to see, specialist decisions created queues, and customer or supplier waiting time was often attributed to internal teams. The group needed a shared view of where the service was really getting stuck.

Structure
Shift & Share
Perspectives
Six service experiments
Output
One combined improvement plan

The stations

Six practical ways to see and reduce service delays

Each station shared an experiment, then gathered questions and feedback from colleagues rotating around the room.

Lead-time radar

Map the complete journey and measure working time separately from time spent waiting between stages.

Ready-to-start standard

Use a simple intake checklist and stop incomplete requests entering the workflow before they create rework.

Delay classification

Record consistent causes—from missing information to approval waits—so recurring patterns become visible.

Bottleneck busting

Map work by role, then use cross-training and delegated decisions to remove single points of failure.

External dependency tracking

Separate internal delays from time spent waiting for customers and suppliers, with clearer escalation routes.

Visual workflow management

Show every job’s current stage so queues, blocked work and ownership are visible to everyone involved.

What changed

The evidence reframed delay as a system-flow problem

  • In one process map, work was active for only about 20% of its lifespan; the rest was waiting.
  • Many delays began before work formally started because requests were incomplete.
  • One specialist was involved in almost 40% of delayed work, exposing a single point of failure.
  • Nearly a third of outstanding work was waiting on external parties.
  • Simple visibility and process changes could be trialled before adding staff or buying new software.

The outcome

A six-part service-flow improvement plan

Instead of selecting a single experiment, the group connected the strongest ideas into a practical approach for understanding the service and targeting improvement without assigning blame.

  1. 01

    Map the end-to-end customer journey and measure waiting as well as working time.

  2. 02

    Introduce a clear ready-to-start checklist for incoming work.

  3. 03

    Categorise delay causes consistently and review the patterns.

  4. 04

    Visualise all work in progress, including blocked and waiting work.

  5. 05

    Identify overloaded roles and reduce bottlenecks through cross-training or delegation.

  6. 06

    Report internal and external waiting separately to clarify ownership and expectations.

“The service suffers primarily from waiting, handoffs, unclear ownership and bottlenecks—not from the actual execution of work.”

— Group learning summary
