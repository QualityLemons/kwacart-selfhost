Case study · Stakeholder opposition

Turning opposition into a test of visible commitment

A project team used Wicked Questions and Generative Relationships STAR to understand why teachers and parents remained cautious about a proposed Environmental Innovation Hub—and what businesses needed to do next.

The challenge

Support the school without leaving it another programme to run

The educational and environmental case was compelling, but teachers and parents were concerned about workload, reliability and whether business support would last. Explaining the benefits again would not answer those concerns. The team needed to design for both school ownership and dependable outside support.

Structures
Two connected sessions
Central tension
Ownership and support
Outcome
A visible commitment plan

Part one · Wicked Questions

Replace either-or arguments with tensions the design must hold

The group generated pairs of apparently opposing needs, then refined the six tensions most important to a credible three-year programme.

Ownership and delivery

Let schools own educational outcomes while businesses carry much of the organising and delivery effort.

Ambition and available time

Create meaningful environmental impact with only a few volunteer hours available each month.

Partnership and resilience

Welcome broad business support while ensuring the core programme survives fluctuating participation.

Evidence and workload

Measure environmental outcomes without adding significant administration for teachers.

Pupil leadership and continuity

Give pupils genuine ownership while adults provide structure, safety and sustained support.

Innovation and reliability

Refresh projects each year within a stable framework that communities can trust for three years.

The pivotal question

How can the school own the Hub while businesses carry much of the burden simultaneously?

  • Schools lead educational priorities and retain ownership of learning outcomes.
  • Businesses coordinate volunteers, activities, evidence gathering and reporting.
  • Pupils generate ideas while adults provide mentoring, resources and safeguarding.
  • A stable core programme protects continuity; new challenges keep it fresh.
  • Additional partners strengthen the programme without becoming critical dependencies.

Part two · Generative Relationships STAR

Diagnose why a strong idea was not yet earning trust

The STAR assessment showed abundant perspectives and a compelling shared reason for acting. Alignment was only moderate, however, and action was notably weaker. The opposition was not evidence that stakeholders rejected the purpose; it reflected reasonable doubt about delivery.

The outcome

Six visible commitments to replace reassurance with evidence

The group stopped generating more concepts and defined early actions that would let teachers and parents judge the partnership by what it did.

  1. 01

    Appoint a project lead and named owners for each major activity.

  2. 02

    Recruit an initial volunteer team and publish a realistic monthly time commitment.

  3. 03

    Listen directly to teachers and parents before finalising the programme.

  4. 04

    Invite local businesses to adopt specific activities and spread delivery risk.

  5. 05

    Publish a Year 1 calendar so stakeholders can see when support will arrive.

  6. 06

    Book one achievable environmental activity to demonstrate progress within a month.

“The quickest way to build trust is not to explain our commitment. It is to demonstrate it.”

— Group conclusion

Use the pattern

Treat opposition as information about what your plan still needs

Use Wicked Questions to hold competing needs together, then Generative Relationships STAR to identify which relationship conditions need practical attention.
